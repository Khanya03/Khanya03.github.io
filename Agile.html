About the project

Fundi Shop is a verified-trades marketplace: buy from the person who does the work.

Customers find a verified tradesperson (a fundi: electrician, plumber, handyman), buy materials and book labour in one cart, pay securely, and review the job once it is complete. Two paths are supported: Just Buy (materials only) and Buy + Book (materials and labour). The business model earns revenue from commission on material sales, storefront subscriptions, per-job booking fees and featured placement.

Technology: Java Spring Boot REST API with a layered architecture (domain, factory, repository, service, controller) and the Builder pattern, MySQL, React (Vite) frontend, JWT security, hosted on AWS.

1. Agile Team Charter

The shared contract for how the Fundi Shop team works.

Evidence

Purpose & visionBuild FundiShop, a platform where customers buy materials and book labour from the same verified tradesperson. Success means a customer can find a verified fundi, pay for materials and labour in one checkout, and review the completed job; fundis can run a storefront; admins can verify and moderate.
Team members & rolesChris Hakizimana — Team member; Christian Hakizimana — Frontend Developer; Emmanuel Posholi Posholi — Team member; Kabelo Nimrod Moloko — Team member; Lisakhanya Mpahla — Backend Developer; Muhammad Siddeeq Rabin — Team member.
Agile frameworkScrum-style one-week sprints (Tuesday to Friday) tracked on a Trello Kanban board: Product Backlog list, then one list per sprint.
CeremoniesSprint planning at the start of each sprint (sprint goal card plus selected stories); daily stand-up recorded in the Agile Logbook (three questions: what I completed, what I plan next, what blocks me); review/demo at the end of the sprint to show completed work and gather feedback; retrospective to reflect on what went well and what needs improvement.
Working agreementsWork is broken into user stories with task checklists; a story is done only when every task on its checklist is ticked; blockers are raised early; the board is kept up to date; team members communicate regularly and meet deadlines; work is reviewed before it is marked complete.
CommunicationTrello for task tracking, GitHub for code and version control, WhatsApp for quick updates and daily communication, and email for formal communication and project-related queries.
Decision-makingTeam decisions were made through group discussion on WhatsApp and calls, where everyone shared their views before agreeing on the final direction. If a decision needed clarification or a blocker could not be resolved internally, it was escalated to the lecturer for guidance.
Definition of DoneAll tasks on the story checklist complete; code runs end-to-end (request → controller → service → repository → database); tested; story ticked on Trello.
ValuesRespect, transparency, accountability, trust, and a learning mindset.
Risks & constraintsScope creep (the business model has many revenue features); teammates not communicating; starting work late; fixed delivery deadline; free or low-cost hosting.

Reflection

The charter helped us agree on how the team would work, what we needed to deliver, and how we would handle problems as they came up. It was useful because it gave us a shared structure for sprint planning, communication, and review, but it also showed a gap between what we agreed and what happened in practice. We said we would communicate regularly and meet deadlines, yet some group members did not communicate consistently, which created delays and made progress less predictable. That gap taught me that a team charter is only valuable when the agreements are reviewed and reinforced during the sprint, not just written once at the start. Next time I would review the charter at the end of each sprint retrospective, check which agreements were actually followed, and give each agreement a visible owner so the team can hold itself accountable.

Sprint 1: Backend Architecture Foundation 2 stories

As a developer, I want the database schema designed and built in MySQL before any backend layer is coded, so that transactional integrity is guaranteed from the start.

  • Design entity-relationship diagram (users, storefronts, listings, orders, bookings, reviews) in MySQL Workbench
  • Implement database schema and migrations
  • Set up relationships and constraints
  • Test schema with sample data and queries

As a developer, I want the backend built with Java Spring Boot using a layered architecture - domain, factory, repository, service, controller - with the Builder pattern for every domain object, so that business logic and APIs are robust, consistent, and scalable.

  • Domain layer — build domain classes (User, Fundi, Storefront, Listing, Order, Booking, Review, etc.), each with a private constructor and a static Builder inner class
  • Factory layer — build Factory classes that use the Builders to construct and validate domain objects before they reach the service layer
  • Repository layer — build Spring Data JPA repository interfaces for each domain entity, wired to the MySQL schema
  • Service layer — build Service classes containing business logic, calling repositories and using factories to construct return objects
  • Controller layer — build REST Controllers exposing endpoints for auth, listings, orders and bookings; test the full request → controller → service → repository → domain chain end-to-end

Reflection

The stories I worked on were the backend foundation and the planning discussions around the early product backlog and sprint goals. I learned that user stories are most useful when they are clear and tied to a real user need, because they help the team focus on value rather than just technical tasks. I also learned that a story is easier to complete when it is broken into small, manageable tasks and when the team keeps updating the board with progress and blockers.

The weaker stories are the technical ones ("As a developer, I want the database schema designed…"), which describe work rather than user value, although they were necessary for the foundation sprint. The stories also have no acceptance criteria and no assigned owner on the card, so it was not always clear who was responsible for what. I learned from this that the team needed clearer user value and stronger ownership, and it helped me understand how to keep the backlog practical and easy to work from. Next time I would add acceptance criteria and a named owner to every story.

Reflection

The backlog is ordered by dependency and value. The database and backend come first, then the frontend and AWS hosting, then accounts and fundi verification, because trust is the product's main promise. Listings, rates, search and cart follow, and then checkout, booking, payments, reviews and the admin dashboard. Revenue features (commission, subscriptions, booking fees, featured placement) are placed in Sprints 9 and 12, after the core buy-and-book loop works. Sprint 10 is marked as the MVP checkpoint, and Sprint 13 holds the remaining "Could-have" stories, which shows we ranked stories by importance.

What I would improve: the board has no visible priority labels, story points or estimates, so the order is the only priority signal, and the sprint dates were planned for 13 weeks without a way to adjust them as we learned our real speed. The order was agreed through team discussion on WhatsApp and calls, and I would move the featured placement and subscription stories later because they depend on the core marketplace working first. Next time I would add MoSCoW labels and story points, and re-order the backlog after every sprint review.

Reflection

Sprint 1 had a clear goal: build the Spring Boot backend with a strict layered architecture and the Builder pattern on top of a MySQL schema designed first, with no frontend work. We committed to two stories and nine tasks, and all nine are ticked complete on the board. Keeping the sprint to one theme made it easy to say whether we had reached the goal. Sprint 2 then planned the React frontend, AWS hosting, security and the first account story.

An honest weakness: the Sprint 1 tasks were all ticked on 6 and 7 August, after the sprint had ended on 31 July, and Sprints 2 to 5 show no ticks at all even though later work exists in the code. The board therefore lagged behind the real progress, which weakens it as a record. In Sprint 1, I built the backend foundation with the layered architecture and the Builder pattern, and I learned that a strong core design makes later features easier to add. Next time I would tick tasks as they are completed, and review the sprint against its goal on the last day.

Reflection

The logbook made me answer three questions every day: what I completed, what I plan next, and what is blocking me. Two patterns stand out in my entries. First, my impediments were about time, such as "start on time" and "not finishing on time", and in the later week I wrote that I needed to add fewer features, which shows I was becoming aware of scope creep. Second, I recorded that some group mates did not communicate, which was the team's biggest risk. Between the two weeks the website went from incomplete to finished, and the team moved on to adding pictures and a presentation.

The individual daily entries carry no dates, even though the weekly logbook sections are dated. This means the exact day-by-day record is not fully traceable, and the logbook is weaker than it should be because some entries describe attending class rather than project work. One real blocker I raised was that some group members did not communicate and that we were starting the website work too late. We reduced the problem by meeting more regularly, discussing the work clearly on WhatsApp, and splitting the remaining website tasks, although the later logbook shows communication problems continued. After that, the website moved from incomplete to finished and we were able to focus on the final presentation and pictures.

Connect: how the five artefacts fit together

The charter set how the team would work. The product backlog turned the Fundi Shop idea into ordered user stories, and each story was broken into tasks. Each sprint pulled a few stories into a sprint backlog with a goal, and the logbook recorded the daily progress and blockers in between. The weak points I found in one artefact, such as the board lagging behind the work and the lack of dates on the individual daily entries, show up in the others, and they all point to the same lesson: Agile records are only valuable when they are kept up to date, specific and shared by the whole team.
